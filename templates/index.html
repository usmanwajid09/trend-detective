Why you can trust a coloured cell

Mann-Kendall + Sen's slope

Uses only the order of values, and the slope is the median of all pairwise slopes — one odd year cannot fake a trend. Every slope carries a 95% confidence interval.

Autocorrelation correction

When one year resembles the last, the data hold less independent information than their length suggests, so the test widens its uncertainty (Hamed & Rao, first 3 lags).

False-discovery control

Testing thousands of cells at p < 0.05 flags hundreds by luck alone. Benjamini-Hochberg turns p-values into q-values; a coloured cell has q ≤ 0.05.

The honest complication: for Lahore, NASA POWER says no significant warming (−0.061 °C/decade, p = 0.53) while the GISTEMP cell says it is warming (+0.26 °C/decade, p ≈ 1×10⁻⁵) — and all eight neighbouring cells agree. Two real NASA datasets, two different answers for the same city.

Data: NASA POWER (power.larc.nasa.gov), NASA GISTEMP v4 (data.giss.nasa.gov), GPCP v2.3 (psl.noaa.gov). Point trends are ordinary least-squares regressions with a two-sided p-value. Map trends are Mann-Kendall tests with Sen's slope, a Hamed-Rao autocorrelation correction (first 3 lags), and Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery control at 5%. “Significant” means p < 0.05 for a point series and q ≤ 0.05 for a map cell.
